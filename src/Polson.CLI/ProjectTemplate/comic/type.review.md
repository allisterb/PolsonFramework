## Where the panel comes from: review

**There is no reference image. The panel is yours to invent, from the brief.**

That makes the director's comments the only external signal you get, so the loop is: you complete a
stage, you render it, they respond in words, and their response shapes the next stage. Their channel
is text — they cannot draw here — so treat what they say as a contribution to the panel rather than
as approval or disapproval of it.

### Stop at the stage boundaries

Each of the three production stages ends somewhere the director can usefully look, and the value of
this type comes from actually stopping there:

- **After `Pencil`** — the cheapest moment in the whole project to be told the composition is wrong.
  A pose or a camera angle changed here costs one script; changed after `Colour` it costs the colour
  pass as well.
- **After `Colour`** — palette and light direction are settled, and both are expensive to revisit
  once ink is over them.
- **After `Ink`** — the last point before the critique.

Render, say in one or two sentences what you were going for and what you are unsure about, then
**ask the director a question and wait for the answer** — do not begin the next stage until it comes
back. Give it two to four options so it can be answered with one click, and leave room for something
you did not anticipate.

Do not ask them to approve; ask them to rule something *out*, or to say which of two readings the
panel gives them. "Does this work?" invites a yes that tells you nothing.

If the question goes unanswered you will be told so: choose, record the choice in a `Stage.note`, and
ask again at the next boundary. One unanswered question is a director who stepped away, not
permission to finish the panel alone.

### Show, do not describe

Where a decision is genuinely open — two compositions, two light directions, two treatments of a
face — render both small and put them side by side rather than describing them. Asking a director to
choose between two sentences is asking them to imagine; asking them to choose between two renders is
asking them to look, which they can actually do.

`Layout.columns(...)` makes a two-up or three-up comparison sheet cheap, and it costs one script.

### Invention is the job, and it needs a stated intent

Without a reference there is nothing to measure yourself against, which is the standing risk of this
type: a panel can be competently drawn and be about nothing. Before the `Pencil` stage, write down
and log:

```
SUBJECT:  <what is happening in this panel>
CAMERA:   <where the viewer is standing, and why that position>
MOMENT:   <the instant chosen — usually just before or just after the event, not during it>
```

The `MOMENT` line is the one that does the work. A panel showing the action itself is often the
weakest choice available; the beat before or after is what makes a reader supply the rest.

Then hold yourself to it in `Critique`: the question is not only "is this well drawn" but "is this
still the moment I said I was drawing".
