## Style direction: geometric

The director has set the stylistic frame. This is Manual 12 §2.5b — temperature, weight and shape
language — **not** the archetype. Which archetype carries it (pictorial, letterform, monogram,
negative space…) is still yours to choose at Stage 4, once you have candidate forms to look at.

Build the mark from **circles, arcs and straight lines with stated relationships**, not from freehand
curves that happen to look tidy. Every centre, radius and tangent should be derivable from the
armature rather than nudged into place — and a reader should be able to see the construction in your
staged artifacts even though it is absent from the final mark.

- Work on a **declared armature**: golden circles, a polar grid, or an isometric grid. Choose it in
  Stage 3 and say why in a `Stage.note`.
- Prefer **tangency and shared centres** to coincidence. Where two curves meet, they should meet
  cleanly — the toolkit has a tangent-blend call that computes the fillet exactly rather than
  approximating it by eye.
- Keep the **stroke weight systematic**: one weight, or weights in a stated ratio. An arbitrary third
  weight is the fastest way for a geometric mark to stop reading as constructed.
- Geometry that is *measured* still needs to be **optically corrected**. A circle that meets a flat
  edge needs overshoot; a bar joining two forms needs the bone-effect correction; a mark centred by
  measurement sits low. Apply these deliberately and note where the corrected version looked right
  and the measured one did not — that gap is the whole argument for the optical toolkit.

**What defeats it:** rigidity read as coldness. A purely geometric mark can end up with nothing to
hold the eye. One deliberate incident — an asymmetry, a cut, a counter that does double duty — is
usually what separates a constructed mark from a diagram.
